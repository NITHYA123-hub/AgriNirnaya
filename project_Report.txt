Abstract and Executive Summary
AgriNirnay recommends one best-matched crop for a farmer's parcel and shows what it would cost, earn and break even at, using soil, season, water, weather and mandi-price inputs in a single workflow.
Indian agriculture contributes over 18% of GDP and employs over 45% of the workforce, yet smallholders face low profitability, yield gaps and post-harvest price volatility. Karnataka spans 10 agro-climatic zones, from coastal laterite strips to arid Deccan black soils, and crop choices there are still often made on intuition, peer imitation or incomplete information.
The platform is built in Python and Streamlit. It combines a consolidated crop dataset, a multi-criteria scoring model, Open-Meteo weather, APMC market-price information, soil-report assistance (PDF/OCR) and a financial estimator with conservative, expected and optimistic scenarios. The recommendation is a transparent rule-based ranking, not a trained prediction model, and the system supports evaluation of options rather than guaranteeing yield or profit.
Keywords: Agriculture, Crop Recommendation, Data Science, Karnataka, Soil Health, Weather Forecasting, APMC Prices, Financial Estimation, Streamlit.
1. Introduction and Motivation
A crop that performs well in one district may fail in another because of differences in rainfall, soil, temperature, irrigation and local practice. Before sowing, a farmer must weigh soil characteristics, nutrient availability, season, water, crop duration, expected yield, input costs and nearby market prices.
This information exists in government publications, agricultural university guidelines (UAS package of practices), weather services, mandi databases and soil-testing laboratories, but in separate silos and technical formats that smallholders find hard to interpret. A digital decision-support system can organise these data into one understandable workflow.
AgriNirnay's motivation is twofold:
• Replace long, unranked crop lists with one leading candidate and an option to inspect alternatives.
• Show the gap between estimated revenue and cultivation cost before a farmer commits resources.
The project aligns with the SFIC 2026 theme, Samriddh Annadata, Samriddh Bharat, by applying data science and accessible technology to crop planning.

6. Crop Recommendation Methodology
The engine in crop_recommendation.py ranks candidate crops with a weighted five-factor suitability score, then spotlights the top-ranked one. It is a rule-based model, not a trained or probability-calibrated predictor.
S_{total} = S_{district} + S_{season} + S_{water} + S_{soil} + S_{pH}
6.1 Scoring Components
Factor
Range (pts)
Rule
District adaptation
0 to +40 (−30 for out-of-region plantation trees)
Proven native district crops score +40; plantation trees unsuited to the region are penalised so high prices alone cannot lift them
Season compatibility
−25 to +30
Kharif crop in Kharif +30; opposite season −25; suitable annual or perennial crops score per defined rules
Water supply fit
−35 to +20
Low water: Paddy and Sugarcane −35, drought-hardy millets and pulses +20
Soil texture
up to +10
Full match +10, smaller score for a partial match
Soil pH
+5 / −5
+5 if farm pH is within the crop's min–max range, −5 otherwise; a missing pH is treated as unavailable, not as a failed match
S_{final} = \min\left(98, \max\left(15, S_{total}\right)\right)
The displayed score is capped to the range 15–98, which is why many top regional results show 98%. The exact weights must match the current implementation of crop_recommendation.py.
6.2 Single-Crop Spotlight
1. Filter candidate crops using the farm details.
2. Score each candidate and sort by score.
3. Show the highest-ranked eligible crop in a spotlight card with yield, price, cost and suitability factors.
4. Let the user step through alternatives one at a time.
6.3 Interpretation
A high score means a crop matches the factors the model represents; it is not the probability of success. Normalisation should not hide serious mismatches, such as a wrong season or insufficient water. Soil depth, pest pressure, seed variety, irrigation reliability and management practice are not modelled, so the result is a best match under available information and a starting point for evaluation.
7. Soil, Weather and Market Modules
Three supporting modules feed the recommendation and finance pages: soil-report processing (soil_reader.py), weather (weather.py) and mandi intelligence (market_data.py).
7.1 Soil Health and Report Processing
Users can upload a PDF, JPG or PNG soil report. Digital PDFs allow direct text extraction through PyPDF; scanned PDFs and photos need OCR through Pillow and PyTesseract.
1. Accept a supported file and check its extension and size.
2. Extract text directly when readable; otherwise apply OCR.
3. Identify candidate values such as pH, nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.
4. Show the values for user review and correction.
5. Use only reviewed values in later calculations.
OCR can misread digits, decimal points and units, and laboratory layouts differ. The module is built to import safely and handle a missing Tesseract binary, unreadable files or unsupported formats without crashing the app. An extracted number is not assumed accurate, and the module does not replace a certified laboratory test.
7.2 Weather Forecasting
The Open-Meteo client displays current temperature, humidity, wind, precipitation and conditions, plus daily minimum and maximum temperatures and forecast rainfall and precipitation probability where the API returns them. A current precipitation of 0.0 mm does not mean no rain later in the day, so current readings, daily totals and probabilities are shown separately, with location and forecast date. Network failures must produce a clear error or labelled fallback, never silently stale values presented as live.
7.3 APMC Market-Price Intelligence
Each market record should carry commodity, market, district, observation date, minimum, modal and maximum price, unit, and source. Records are filtered by the selected crop and district so that, for example, grain and pulse pages no longer show statewide Arecanut prices. If no local record exists, the application says so rather than substituting another crop or district. A modal price is a reference, not a guaranteed selling price: transport, handling, commission, quality and post-harvest losses all affect what a farmer receives, and sample or historical data must not be labelled live.
8. Financial Estimation and Scenario Analysis
financial_estimates.py scales per-acre baselines by farm area (A), yield (Y) and price (P) to give cost, revenue, net return, ROI and break-even values. These are planning estimates, not guaranteed outcomes.
8.1 Formulas
Cultivation cost combines seed, fertiliser or manure, irrigation and plant protection, and harvesting labour:
C_{acre} = C_{seed} + C_{fertiliser} + C_{irrigation/protection} + C_{labour/harvest}, \quad C_{total} = C_{acre} \times A
Q_{total} = Y_{acre} \times A, \qquad R_{gross} = Q_{total} \times P, \qquad \Pi_{net} = R_{gross} - C_{total}
ROI(\%) = \frac{\Pi_{net}}{C_{total}} \times 100
P_{BE} = \frac{C_{total}}{Q_{total}}, \qquad Y_{BE} = \frac{C_{acre}}{P}
Yield is in quintals per acre and price in rupees per quintal. Zero or invalid cost, production or price must be handled to avoid division errors, and cost categories must not double-count.
8.2 Three-Scenario Sensitivity
Scenario

The percentages are scenario assumptions, not forecasts from a validated probability model. Yield and price sliders recalculate costs, gross income and net profit immediately.
8.3 What the Estimate Leaves Out
Net return is a simplified figure and may exclude transport, storage, financing, land rent, post-harvest losses and other expenses unless explicitly included. Actual results can differ because of rainfall, pests, disease, seed quality, input-price changes and market volatility.

10. Development Workflow
The project followed nine stages, from requirements to demonstration:
1. Requirement analysis: define users, inputs, outputs and geographic coverage.
2. Dataset preparation: collect records, standardise fields, units and names, record sources and dates.
3. Application design: home page, farm form, soil page, recommendations, weather, market and finance views.
4. Recommendation logic: implement scoring rules and check that rankings respond to district, season, soil and water.
5. External data integration: connect weather and market sources with error handling, source labels and timestamps.
6. Financial calculation: implement cost, revenue, net return, ROI and break-even, verifying units.
7. Integration: confirm farm selections flow correctly to downstream pages.
8. Testing: run functional, data and calculation checks and fix errors.
9. Documentation and demonstration: report, presentation, screenshots and demo workflow.
Code is version-controlled on Git and GitHub, and the application is deployed on Streamlit Community Cloud.

12. Results and Discussion
AgriNirnay delivers a working end-to-end workflow, live at agrinirnaya.streamlit.app, that turns farm inputs into a ranked crop, local price context and a financial forecast.
12.1 Outputs
1. A structured view of the farm's district, area, season and water availability.
2. A top-ranked crop from the suitability rules, with alternatives one click away.
3. Supporting crop information: yield, duration, price and cost.
4. Weather observations and forecasts where the external service responds.
5. District-relevant mandi observations where valid records exist.
6. Estimated cultivation cost, revenue, net return, ROI and break-even values under three scenarios.
12.2 Interpretation
• Recommendation: a high score reflects a match to the modelled factors, not a probability of success; the model omits soil depth, pest pressure, seed variety and irrigation reliability.
• Finance: a positive projected return does not mean the farmer will earn that amount; actual returns depend on production, selling price, losses and costs outside the calculation.
• Coverage: the 31-district, 270-record scope should be reported as achieved only after the current files are inspected. Incomplete modules should be described as under development, not as finished results.
13. Limitations, Risks and Responsible Use
Each main limitation has a stated mitigation, and the system is positioned as decision support, not a guarantee.
Risk
Why it matters
Mitigation
Data quality
Incomplete, outdated or mislabelled records mislead recommendations and estimates
Keep source metadata, validate units, separate observed data from assumptions
OCR reliability
Scanned, blurry or differently formatted reports fail
User review of extracted values, validation checks, clear fallback
Weather uncertainty
Forecasts change and may not match the exact farm
Show timestamps, location and uncertainty guidance
Market volatility
Prices differ by quality, market and date
Show source, date, market and unit; never label historical data live

Not every expense or production risk is modelled

Rule-based model

Farmer-Centred Principles
• Use understandable labels and familiar units, and explain what drove each recommendation.
• Let users review extracted soil values and avoid asking for data available from a reliable source.
• Mark unavailable, estimated or outdated information, and avoid overstating certainty.
• Encourage consultation with local agricultural experts.
• Protect uploaded documents and avoid unnecessary retention of personal information.
14. Future Enhancements and Expected Impact
The next phase should focus on data quality and validation before adding new features.
14.1 Planned Enhancements
1. Kannada language support: labels, instructions and agricultural terminology.
2. Better soil-report processing: more report formats and stronger validation of pH and N-P-K values.
3. Expanded dataset: verified sources for crop requirements, seasons, costs, yields and market observations.
4. Reliable market integration: a dependable price source with update timestamps and clear handling of delayed data, including AGMARKNET and official market systems where access allows.
5. Validated recommendation rules: check scoring against agricultural references and expert feedback; test a machine-learning model only if enough reliable labelled data exists.
6. Advanced financial scenarios: sensitivity to yield, price, input costs and post-harvest losses.
7. Mobile-friendly interface: optimised for smartphones and low bandwidth.
8. Crop-disease assistance: image-based identification as a separate, evaluated enhancement.
9. Field evaluation: a structured pilot with farmers and agricultural stakeholders before any claims on adoption, yield, cost or income.
14.2 Expected Impact
If implemented and validated, the platform could support more structured crop selection, better awareness of soil and water needs, easier comparison of duration and cost, clearer use of market prices and more transparent financial planning. These are expected benefits, not measured outcomes; showing real gains in productivity or income needs field evaluation.
15. Conclusion
AgriNirnay gives Karnataka farmers an intuitive, localised and financially transparent way to compare crop options before sowing. It joins a consolidated master dataset, a multi-criteria scoring model, Open-Meteo weather, APMC price tracking, soil-report assistance and interactive financial modelling in one guided Streamlit workflow.
Its usefulness depends on the accuracy of the dataset, the reliability of external integrations, the validity of the scoring rules and the correctness of the financial calculations. Future work will therefore focus on data quality, expert validation of recommendations, stronger soil-report processing and accessibility for farmers.
AgriNirnay does not promise guaranteed yield or profit. Its objective is to make assumptions visible so farmers can evaluate their options and make better-informed decisions, in line with the SFIC 2026 theme, Samriddh Annadata, Samriddh Bharat.
